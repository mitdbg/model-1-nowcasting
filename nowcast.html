The GDP nowcast

An estimate of the current quarter's real GDP growth, updated every Friday from the economic data published that week. The model is our replica of the New York Fed's Staff Nowcast 2.0, rebuilt from public data.

Every quarter, week by week Explore one quarter How accurate is it? What makes it work The model in detail

How it works, in brief. Many statistics rise and fall together because they respond to the same few forces: the overall pace of the economy, business sentiment, prices and the job market. The model treats these forces as hidden variables (factors), estimates them from 35 monthly and quarterly data series, and reads GDP growth off them. Each week it updates the factors with that week's releases, and each data release moves the nowcast in proportion to how much it surprised the model.

Every nowcast on this page uses only the data that had been published by its date, including the unrevised first versions of each figure. That is the honest test of how the model would have done in real time.

The biggest surprises this quarter

Actual and expected values are in the units the model uses: % changes for most series, changes in thousands for payrolls, index levels for surveys. Each surprise (actual minus expected) moves the nowcast by a weight the model estimates for that series and release; the weight can be positive or negative.

How accurate is it?

We score the nowcast by its mean squared error (MSE): the average of the squared misses, in percentage points of annualized growth. An MSE of 1.4 means a typical miss of about 1.2 points. Lower is better. The yardstick is a simple autoregressive (AR) benchmark, which forecasts GDP growth from its own past alone.

We replayed every quarter from 2006 to 2019, each week using only the data published by then. On the day before the government's first estimate, the nowcast's mean squared error was 1.37, about the same as the New York Fed reports for its own model (1.3) and 65% lower than the benchmark (3.93).

Against the nowcasts the New York Fed actually published

From 2016 to 2019 the New York Fed published its (older) model's nowcast every week. On the same dates, our replica had smaller errors against BEA's first estimate, with p-values near 0.05. Against the latest revised GDP figures the difference disappears. These were calm years, and the simple AR benchmark did about as well as either model.

What makes it work

The 2.0 model adds four features to the older design. Switching each one off in turn shows which matter: a slowly drifting trend in long-run growth, and outlier scales that stop a single odd month from moving the estimate. With all four off, the model is no better than the older one.

The model in detail

The model has four general factors (a global factor that every real-activity series loads on, and factors for surveys, nominal series such as prices and dollar values, and labor), a factor that exists only during the 2020 pandemic, and a slowly moving trend in long-run growth. The Model structure page shows which series load on which factor.

For economistsEquations

For a standardized monthly series \(i\):

$$y_{it} = \iota_i\, g_t + \lambda_i' f_t + \lambda^c_i\, c_t + e_{it}$$

Quarterly series (GDP, GDI, unit labor cost) are observed every third month as a weighted sum of the unobserved monthly values (Mariano and Murasawa 2003):

$$y^q_{it} = \tfrac{1}{9}\,(1, 2, 3, 2, 1) \cdot \big(\iota_i g + \lambda_i' f + e^q_i\big)_{t,\,t-1,\,\dots,\,t-4}$$

The trend, the factors and the series-specific terms evolve as

$$g_t = g_{t-1} + \gamma_g v_t, \qquad f_t = \sum_{l=1}^{4} \Phi_l f_{t-l} + s^f_t\, \sigma^f_t\, \varepsilon^f_t, \qquad e_{it} = \phi_i e_{i,t-1} + s^e_{it}\, \sigma^e_{it}\, \varepsilon_{it}$$ $$\ln (\sigma_t)^2 = \ln (\sigma_{t-1})^2 + \gamma\, \eta_t, \qquad s_t \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}, \qquad c_t = s^c_t\, \varepsilon^c_t \text{ in March–September 2020, else } 0$$
  • \(g_t\): trend in long-run growth, loading 1 on GDP and GDI only.
  • \(f_t\): global, soft, nominal and labor factors; every series loads on global except the price indexes, and each also loads on its group's factor.
  • \(\sigma\) and \(s\): each shock has its own random-walk volatility and, each month, an outlier scale of 1 (usual) or 2–5.
For economistsEstimation, data and departures from the paper
  • Sampler (paper, appendix A): the state by the simulation smoother; loadings and AR coefficients by Bayesian regression; the VAR kept stationary; volatilities by Kim, Shephard and Chib (1998) with the 10-component mixture of Omori et al. (2007); outlier scales from their discrete posterior. 1,500 burn-in sweeps, 1,000 kept, thinned to 100 draws; about 11 minutes per fit.
  • State: 45 elements (trend, 4 factors, COVID factor and 3 quarterly series-specific terms, each with 5 lags). Monthly series-specific terms are quasi-differenced out of the state.
  • Parameters: about 270, plus a volatility and an outlier scale for each of 39 shocks in every month.
  • Backtest: re-estimated on the first Wednesday of each quarter on data from January 1985; nowcast every Friday; the nowcast is the posterior mean over the kept draws.
  • Data: the 35 series of the paper's list (the older model's list less capacity utilization and the PPI), with Dallas and Philadelphia Fed survey indexes in place of the ISM indexes, which are not on FRED. ALFRED vintages for every series that has them; values from before a series' archive begins are left out unless the series is barely revised (surveys, price indexes).
  • Departures: the paper does not publish its prior values, so ours are our own (loadings and AR coefficients N(0, 1); trend volatility 0.025 with 1,000 prior observations, which the data barely move; outlier probability about one month in 48). Price indexes do not load on the global factor, and quarterly loadings are drawn with their series-specific terms integrated out; both were needed for the model to fit. Standardization leaves out March–December 2020.
