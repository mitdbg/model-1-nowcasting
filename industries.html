The idea: a few hidden drivers behind many series

Hundreds of economic statistics rise and fall together, because they respond to the same few underlying forces: the business cycle, inflation pressure, interest rates, and conditions in a particular industry. The models on this page treat these forces as hidden variables, called factors, and estimate them from the data. Each series is explained as a weighted mix of the factors plus a part of its own. Forecasting the factors forward then gives a forecast of every series.

How this relates to the GDP nowcast. The GDP nowcast is built on the same idea, but it is a separate model with a different job. It estimates a single number, this quarter's GDP growth, from 35 hand-picked national series, and reports its uncertainty. The models on this page forecast many monthly series, 0 to 12 months ahead, from much larger data sets. They test whether detailed industry data improve forecasts.

How to read the scores. Each model is compared with an autoregressive (AR) benchmark, which forecasts a series from its own recent past. We report the model's error relative to the AR's: "10% better" means errors 10% smaller (a relative root mean squared error of 0.90). The horizon is how many months ahead: 0 is the first month whose data are not yet published (a nowcast), 1 the month after, and so on.

National indicators · general and economy models

Here the general model and the economy model forecast the main national series, without and with the 13 industry factors. On national indicators the general model alone is about level with the AR benchmark. Its forecasts help in recessions but lose a little in long expansions, and 2013–2026 is mostly expansion. Averaging a model's forecast with the AR's ("+ AR") usually beats both. With all 13 industry factors, the economy model combined with the AR beats the AR on industrial production at every horizon.

The model in detail

For economistsEquations

Each series \(i\), transformed to be stationary and standardized, is a common part driven by the factors plus a series-specific part:

$$x_{it} = \lambda_i^{g\prime} g_t + \lambda_i^{s}\, s_{k(i),t} + e_{it}$$ $$f_t = A_1 f_{t-1} + A_2 f_{t-2} + u_t, \qquad f_t = (g_t', s_t')', \qquad u_t \sim N(0, Q)$$ $$e_{it} = \rho_i\, e_{i,t-1} + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad \varepsilon_{it} \sim N(0, \sigma_i^2)$$
  • \(g_t\): the 4 general factors; \(s_{k,t}\): the factor of industry \(k\).
  • Loadings are restricted: FRED-MD series load only on the general factors; an industry series loads on those and on its own industry's factor.
  • The industry models have the same form with 6 factors: the 4 general ones plus an industry activity factor and an industry price factor.
For economistsSize, estimation and testing
  • Economy model: 243 series (126 FRED-MD, 117 industry activity), 810 months (1959-03 to 2026-08, 17.5% of data points missing), 17 factors with 2 lags, a 277-element state and 2,306 estimated parameters.
  • Estimation: maximum likelihood by the EM algorithm with the Kalman filter and smoother (Bańbura and Modugno 2014), which handles missing values and ragged data edges directly.
  • Data handling: FRED-MD's transformation codes and the McCracken–Ng outlier rule (values more than 10 interquartile ranges from the median treated as missing).
  • Choices: 4 general factors and 2 lags were chosen by forecast accuracy over 2000–2012 and then kept fixed, so the later test is not tuned to its own results. Industries get one factor each by design.
  • Real-time test: every month from 2000-01 to 2026-09 (321 forecast dates), the model is fitted on the FRED-MD vintage of that month and forecasts 0 to 12 months ahead. Industry series use their ALFRED archives, which begin between 2010 and 2017. Industry scores on this page count only forecast dates after an industry's archives begin (for the economy model, after all 13 have begun, 2017-11), so every forecast uses data as first published. Industry models are re-fitted every third month.
  • Scores: root mean squared error relative to the AR, on cumulative changes; Diebold–Mariano tests with the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold correction for p-values.
  • Prices: with FRED-MD's default transformation (change in the growth rate), industry price forecasts mostly lose to the AR. The "price t-code 5" comparison uses the plain growth rate for both the model and the AR.
